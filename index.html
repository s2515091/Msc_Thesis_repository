Questions of the method

These are the 23 questions of the method, in the order and wording in which an enterprise architect answers them about their own project. The questions are grouped in five sections. The section on the organizations is shown in one of two versions, depending on the answer to Q7; both versions are given below. Every question ends with the option “I am not sure / not decided yet”, which counts as unanswered.

Instruction shown to the architect. Answer for the project as it has been agreed today: what is already running, and what has been agreed but not yet built. Do not answer for ideas that have not been agreed; choose "I am not sure / not decided yet" instead.

Data

This section is about the data itself. What the data is, what state it is in when it leaves your side, where it came from, how sensitive it is, and how often it has to be refreshed all put limits on how it can be shared and on what has to be arranged around it. Answer for the data in this specific project rather than for your organization as a whole.

Q1

Which of the following categories describe the data the project shares, or will share?

Several answers possible, with a free-text "Other" option

  • Process data: data from organizational processes, research or development (for example, production line logs, maintenance records, R&D test results)
  • Product data: data from smart products or sensors, sometimes called Big Data (for example, vibration readings from a pump, GPS traces from a truck fleet, machine telemetry)
  • Environment data: data about the surroundings (for example, weather forecasts, air quality readings, soil or terrain maps)
  • Customer data: data specifically about customers (for example, order history, contact details, subscription or usage behaviour)
  • Metadata: data that describes another dataset (for example, a data catalogue entry, column definitions, the owner and timestamp of a file)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q2

In which state is the data at the moment it is shared?

Several answers possible, with a free-text "Other" option

  • Raw data: exactly as received from the source (for example, unfiltered sensor readings, untouched machine log files)
  • Modified data: data that has been preprocessed (for example, duplicates removed, missing values filled in, units standardized, labels added)
  • Enriched data: the original data plus extra information from outside the original source (for example, sales figures combined with regional demographics, sensor readings joined with weather data)
  • Processed data: data ready to be analysed by algorithms or ML models (for example, a cleaned feature table, a pre-aggregated dataset)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q3

From which sources does the shared data originate?

Several answers possible

  • Generated data: generated by our own operations, sensors and equipment (for example, our own production line, our own machines and vehicles)
  • Acquired data: obtained from data generated by other organizations (for example, bought from a data provider, received from a supplier under an agreement)
  • Customer provided data: collected directly from our own customers (for example, forms customers fill in, survey answers, app usage)
  • Freely available data: data that is openly published and can be used without an agreement (for example, open government datasets, public weather or map data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q4

Which level of privacy does the data require?

One answer

  • Public data: no restrictions apply, and the data may be shared without restriction (for example, published open datasets, public timetables, published environmental readings)
  • Internal data: the data can be shared within the domain, but requires restrictions when shared outside the domain (for example, internal performance reports, team dashboards)
  • Confidential data: as internal data, but more strongly restricted: only authorized personnel may access it (for example, contract terms, pricing, unreleased product designs)
  • Sensitive data: the data contains personally identifiable information, so sharing it requires legal compliance, such as anonymization or pseudonymization (for example, employee records, patient data, customer personal data under GDPR)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q5

How frequently does the data have to be updated once it is shared?

Several answers possible

  • Static: no updates are required after sharing (for example, a one-off historical export, a fixed reference table)
  • Up to date: repeated updates on a schedule (for example, a daily or weekly batch upload)
  • (near) Real time: a live stream or continuous feed (for example, sensor telemetry pushed the moment it is measured)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.

Organization

This section is about why the data is shared and with whom: what the organization seeks from the exchange, which parties take part in it, and what is given or received in return. Answer for this specific project rather than for your organization as a whole.

Q6

What motivates the sharing of this data?

Several answers possible, with a free-text "Other" option

  • Economic: money or efficiency (for example, new revenue from selling data, cost savings, better forecasting)
  • Social and environmental: to contribute to social or environmental goals (for example, sustainability reporting, emissions tracking, corporate social responsibility programmes)
  • Legal: legislation or a regulator requires the data to be shared (for example, GDPR obligations, EU Data Act, regulator or safety reporting)
  • Cultural: to improve the culture around data sharing within the organization (for example, breaking down silos between departments, building trust in shared data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q7

With whom is the data shared, or to be shared?

One answer

  • Intra-organizational: the data stays inside our own organization (for example, between our production, sales, and logistics departments)
  • Same industry: only with organizations in the same industry (for example, a supplier sharing operational data with a manufacturer)
  • Cross-industry: across sector boundaries (for example, sharing meteorology data with agriculture or aerospace companies)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q8

What does your organization expect in return for sharing the data?

Several answers possible, with a free-text "Other" option

  • Financial reward: a monetary return (for example, a licence fee, a price per dataset)
  • Virtual assets: digital assets that cannot be directly converted into cash (for example, platform credits, tokens, points)
  • Data in return: data is received in exchange for the data shared (for example, we share production data, the partner shares market data)
  • Services: additional or new services from the receiving party (for example, a free analytics dashboard, a maintenance or benchmarking service)
  • (Increased) Reputation: greater brand awareness, trust, or credibility (for example, recognized as a trusted partner, publicity as a frontrunner in the sector)
  • Nothing in return: no return is expected (for example, the data is shared because a regulator requires it, or it is published openly)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q9

Which form of compensation is expected for the data?

One answer

  • Free: the data is shared free of charge (for example, internal sharing between departments, open data, legally mandated reporting)
  • Fixed: a single, fixed price, paid at once or in instalments (for example, EUR 5,000 for a dataset)
  • Subscription based: a recurring price every set period (for example, a monthly or yearly access fee)
  • Usage based: the compensation varies with how much the data is used (for example, a price per API call, per record downloaded)
  • Revenue based: compensation based on the revenue generated from the shared data (for example, 8% of the revenue generated from the data, or royalties)
  • Hybrid: a combination of the models mentioned above (for example, a base subscription plus a charge per API call)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.

The Organization (Contingency Factors, Within One Organization)

Shown when Q7 is answered “Intra-organizational” or “I am not sure / not decided yet”.

This section is about the organization in which the project runs: what it emphasizes, how centralized it is, how far its processes are harmonized, and how regulated its market is. Answer for the organization, or for the part of it that the project spans.

Q10

Which performance objective does the organization emphasize most?

One answer

  • Profit: earning more from the current business, mainly by keeping costs down (for example, margin targets, cost-reduction programmes)
  • Asset utilization: getting more out of what the organization already owns (for example, using plants, vehicles, or systems more fully)
  • Growth: expanding into new products, markets, or customers (for example, revenue growth targets, new business lines)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q11

How centralized is the organization, or the part of it that the project spans?

One answer

  • Centralized: most decisions are taken at the top (for example, headquarters sets budgets, priorities, and policies for all units)
  • Decentralized: the business units or regions decide largely for themselves (for example, each division runs its own budget and priorities)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q12

How harmonized are the business processes that the shared data supports, across the parts of the organization?

One answer

  • Globally harmonized: the units follow the same processes (for example, one order-to-cash process for all countries)
  • Local processes: each unit follows its own processes (for example, country-specific sales or registration procedures)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q13

How regulated is the market in which the organization operates?

One answer

  • Highly regulated: the products, processes, or data are subject to extensive sector regulation and reporting (for example, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, banking, energy)
  • Little or no regulation: little or no sector-specific regulation applies beyond general law such as data protection (for example, consulting, software development, non-food retail)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.

The Participating Organizations (Contingency Factors, Across Organizations)

Shown instead when Q7 is answered “Same industry” or “Cross-industry”.

This section is about the participating organizations taken together: what the collaboration aims at, how the collaboration is led, how far the participants' processes are harmonized, and how regulated their markets are.

Q10

What do the participating organizations mainly aim for with the collaboration?

One answer

  • Profit: lowering the cost of what the participants already do (for example, fewer manual document exchanges along a supply chain)
  • Asset utilization: using capacity the participants already have more fully (for example, shared berths, trucks, or warehouse space)
  • Growth: new services or markets the participants could not reach alone (for example, a new service built on the combined data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q11

Apart from the shared data, how is the business collaboration between the participating organizations led?

One answer

  • Centralized: one organization leads the collaboration (for example, a manufacturer and its suppliers, a port authority and the companies in its port)
  • Decentralized: no organization leads, and the participants take part on an equal footing (for example, an association of companies of similar standing)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q12

How harmonized are the business processes that the shared data supports, across the participating organizations?

One answer

  • Globally harmonized: the participants follow the same processes (for example, an agreed industry procedure for booking, ordering, or customs clearance)
  • Local processes: each organization follows its own processes, and only the exchange between them is aligned (for example, each partner plans its own operations and sends the agreed messages)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q13

How regulated are the markets in which the participating organizations operate? If they differ, answer for the most regulated one.

One answer

  • Highly regulated: the products, processes, or data are subject to extensive sector regulation and reporting (for example, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, banking, energy)
  • Little or no regulation: little or no sector-specific regulation applies beyond general law such as data protection (for example, consulting, software development, non-food retail)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.

Network

This section is about how the exchange works in practice: the route the data takes between the parties, where the data sits once it is shared, what has to line up on both sides for it to be understood, how access is regulated, what sets an exchange off, and how many parties are involved.

Q14

By which route is the data exchanged between the parties?

One answer, with a free-text "Other" option

  • Peer to peer: the data passes directly from one party to the other, with no intermediary (for example, our system calls theirs directly, or they call an API we run ourselves)
  • Platform based: the exchange happens through the intermediation of a platform (for example, a shared portal, or a broker that sits between provider and consumer)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q15

Where does the data reside when it is shared?

One answer

  • Centralized: copied into one shared location, from which consumers retrieve it (for example, all departments load their data into one shared database that others query)
  • Distributed: the data stays where it is produced and is accessed at the source through an agreed interface (for example, each domain or organization exposes its own data through a connector or API; nothing is duplicated centrally)
  • Hybrid: some data is copied into a central store, other data stays at the source and is accessed there.
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q16

Which levels of interoperability does the exchange require between the parties?

Several answers possible

  • Legal interoperability: compatible legal frameworks, standards, and regulations (for example, matching contract clauses, GDPR-compliant terms, agreed usage policies)
  • Organizational interoperability: a shared understanding of processes, objectives, and resources (for example, agreed roles, responsibilities, and escalation paths between the parties)
  • Semantic interoperability: consistency and interpretability across diverse sources and systems (for example, a shared vocabulary or ontology, so that “customer” or “downtime” means the same thing on both sides)
  • Technical interoperability: the same technical data exchange across systems (for example, matching formats and protocols, JSON over REST, OPC UA, agreed schemas)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q17

On what basis is access to the data granted?

One answer

  • Agreement based: access terms are defined between you and the other party (for example, a bilateral data sharing contract, an NDA with data clauses)
  • Application based: access is tied to the use of a specific application (for example, only users logged into the shared dashboard can see the data)
  • Trust based: access is granted on the basis of trust (for example, known partners are granted access without a formal contract or technical gate)
  • Open: no access restrictions apply, and anyone may access the data (for example, published as open data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q18

What triggers an exchange of data?

One answer, with a free-text "Other" option

  • On demand: sharing is triggered when the consumer asks for the data (for example, the consumer queries the API when they need it)
  • Event based: triggered by an event specified by the provider (for example, end of day, the dataset reaching a certain size, an alarm or threshold being crossed)
  • Continuous: sharing data in real time or at regular intervals (for example, a live stream, or an automatic push every 15 minutes)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q19

Which pattern do the data sharing relationships follow?

One answer

  • One-to-one: one party shares data with one other party (for example, one supplier sends data to one manufacturer)
  • One-to-many: one party shares data with many other parties (for example, one provider publishes a dataset that many consumers use)
  • Many-to-one: many parties share data with one other party (for example, many suppliers report into one OEM)
  • Many-to-many: many parties share data with many other parties (for example, an ecosystem where every participant both provides and consumes)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q20

How dependent are the parties on one another’s data?

One answer

  • Loosely coupled: each party can continue to operate if the data stops arriving (for example, the shared data improves a forecast, but the process still runs without it)
  • Tightly coupled: the parties depend on each other’s data to operate (for example, a production schedule that cannot be made without the partner’s live data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.

Governance

This section is about control over the data once it has been shared: who decides what may be done with it afterwards, who holds the authority to set the rules for it and see that they are followed, and how decisions about it are reached. These questions are often still open early in a project; please use the “I am not sure / not decided yet” option rather than guessing.

Q21

Once the data has been shared, can the party that provided it still limit what is done with it?

One answer

  • Yes: the provider sets conditions on how the data may be used (for example, usage policies travel with the data, or the receiver may only use it for an agreed purpose)
  • No, the receiver decides: whoever receives the data decides how it is used (for example, the departments hand their data over to a central team, or the participants hand theirs to one company, which then decides what is done with it)
  • No conditions at all: anyone who receives the data may use it freely (for example, the data is published as open data, or made available to every department without conditions)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q22

When a party wants access to the shared data, who decides whether it gets access?

One answer

  • Each party, for its own data: each party decides who may access the data it provides, and any common rules are agreed by the parties together (for example, each domain or organization decides about the data it publishes, and the common standards are agreed in an association in which the parties decide)
  • One party, for all the data: one party decides for everyone, including about data it did not provide (for example, a central data office or the company that runs the exchange decides who may access which data, or a data trustee that holds the data of several providers decides)
  • Each party, within common rules set by someone else: a body that is not one of the parties sets the common rules, and each party decides within them who may access its own data (for example, headquarters sets the data definitions and quality rules for all units, and each unit decides who may use its data)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
Q23

How are decisions about the shared data reached?

One answer

  • Hierarchical: whoever holds the authority decides on their own and passes the decision down (for example, a manager or a board decides and the teams carry it out)
  • Cooperative: whoever holds the authority decides together with the others involved (for example, decisions are taken in working groups or committees in which the parties take part)
  • I am not sure / not decided yet.
